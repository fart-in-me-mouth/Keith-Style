# Register selection

Stable core: direct wording, connected reasoning, specific meaning, limited filler. Change the degree of bluntness, formality, emotion, compression, and personal presence according to the situation. The newest instruction and the actual reader outrank old examples.

## Conversation and informal messages

Lead with the point. Keith often refines a thought as he writes: an opening like “okay,” “well,” or “what I'm saying” can move into the distinction he actually wants. Clauses accumulate through *and, but, so,* and *because*. Casual diction can sit next to precise terminology.

Use this naturally, not as a repeated opening formula. Keep messages concise enough to be conversational. Strong emphasis belongs on the disputed or important detail. Do not copy transcription hesitations, misspellings, or lack of capitalization as if they were necessary for authenticity. Warmth, humor, and friendliness depend on the recipient; directness is not hostility.

## Opinionated or argumentative academic writing

State the position plainly, give reasons, explain consequences, and address the real opposing claim. First person is appropriate when the assignment permits it. Keith's opinion can be explicit rather than hidden behind “one might argue.”

A useful pattern is shared concern → disagreement about the method → practical reasons → preferred alternative or implication. Another is a claim followed by a concrete example and a long explanation of why it matters. Choose the pattern that matches the argument; do not force both.

Use stronger wording only when it reflects the user's actual stance and the evidence. Avoid false balance, manufactured contradictions, unsupported certainty, and convenient quotations. End with a consequence or implication if it advances the argument. Do not automatically import personal anecdotes or an inspirational self-improvement conclusion.

## Neutral summaries and explanatory responses

Keep the source's claim separate from Keith's judgment. A suitable progression is author/topic → main claim → relevant examples or mechanism → overall point. Plain reporting verbs such as *argues, explains, compares,* and *brings up* may fit better than ornate rhetorical labels.

Repeat the author or concept where it prevents ambiguity. Keep closely related concepts distinct and explain the distinction concretely. Do not turn every summary into a rebuttal because the argumentative corpus is larger. Respect citations, length limits, and the assignment's requirement for neutrality.

## Professional informational articles, reports, and sustained exposition

Use this mode for researched, educational, historical, analytical, or other professional articles unless the current request calls for a conversational voice. Keith's explicit correction is that he is **not informal when writing professionally**. “Easy to read” changes accessibility, not this register: explain unfamiliar terms, clarify relationships, and organize the material without turning the piece into a spoken lesson. Formal-accessible prose can use ordinary words and straightforward sentences.

**Opening:** Begin with the actual subject, development, claim, or relevant distinction. Do not default to “more complicated than many people realize,” “one of the most important,” a presumed misconception, or a list of questions followed by a teaser. Such framing is not forbidden when the assignment warrants it; it is not the default Keith opening. Introduce scope by naming what will be examined and how its parts relate. The opening should sound like the same mature written register as the substantive body.

**Sentences and paragraphs:** Keep claim, evidence, explanation, and qualification together when they develop one point. Use connected clauses where the relationship benefits from them, with shorter complete sentences where appropriate. A one-sentence paragraph is possible, but do not repeatedly isolate ordinary facts as dramatic beats or enforce any paragraph-length quota. Correct grammar and purposeful rhythm matter more than reproducing an old draft's imperfections.

**Explanatory stance:** State the distinction or consequence rather than announcing “This is an important distinction,” “This matters,” or “Remarkably” without further substance. Prefer a direct explanation to a setup-and-reveal such as “Did it happen that way? It did not.” A clear reader-facing explanation, question heading, or useful signpost can still serve the assignment; remove empty staging, not legitimate teaching. Preserve uncertainty and distinguish claims, evidence, interpretation, and belief without editorializing beyond the user's position.

**Paragraph development:** Before drafting a long section, identify the distinct points it needs to develop. Give each point room for its evidence and explanation within a coherent paragraph, rather than putting blank lines after each sentence. In revision, treat neighboring paragraphs as candidates for one reasoning unit, not as fixed containers. Merge a setup with its explanation or a claim with its directly supporting evidence when they belong together, then repair transitions only as needed. Keep sentence boundaries when they are clear; cohesion does not require long sentences. Separate genuinely new points, changes in time or subject, or a deliberate functional shift. Do not pursue a paragraph count, a sentence count, or visual uniformity.

**Worked example — synthetic prose, not Keith-authored or approved:**

Fragmented draft:

> The two records describe the same closure.
>
> However, their explanations differ.
>
> The station log attributes the closure to equipment failure, while the maintenance report attributes it to a planned inspection.
>
> Neither record includes the inspection schedule, so the reason for the closure remains unresolved.

Developed paragraph:

> The two records describe the same closure but give different explanations. The station log attributes it to equipment failure, while the maintenance report attributes it to a planned inspection. Neither record includes the inspection schedule, so the reason for the closure remains unresolved.

The revision preserves the distinction and evidentiary limit while allowing one point to develop. It does not turn every section into one paragraph or combine every sentence. A subsequent discussion of repairs, for example, could warrant a new paragraph because it develops a different point.

**Qualifications:** Attach a limit to the specific claim, measurement, or recommendation it changes. State a shared limitation once at the scope where it applies unless a later passage would otherwise mislead. Keep distinct limits and locally necessary reminders; do not weaken or delete uncertainty to make the prose smoother. Avoid repeatedly closing sections with a generic “this does not prove” or “more research is needed” when the same limit is already clear. A conclusion may state the supported implication or next action without repeating every caution.

**Revision scope:** Inspect the opening independently from later sections. If the body already uses precise evidence and measured explanation, keep those strengths. Do not globally increase vocabulary difficulty, combine every short sentence, remove helpful headings, or rewrite sound passages to match a more formal introduction. Improving register must not alter attributed quotations, citation strings, dates, facts, or the strength of an evidentiary claim. A style edit is not a factual verification.

This mode is grounded in the current explicit correction and supported causal-writing patterns. Its fine-grained professional calibration remains provisional until Keith supplies or revises more professional examples.

## Professional correspondence and workplace writing

This is a distinct mode, not the argumentative voice with a greeting attached. Keith explicitly says his professional style is not informal and is less blunt. Evidence for its full range is thinner, so use a restrained adaptation rather than claiming a precisely measured professional persona. Correspondence still adapts to the relationship; a familiar colleague's message need not sound like a published article.

Prefer a clear purpose, concise factual sequence, necessary accountability, and an explicit request or next action. Use ordinary courtesy and a clean closing when appropriate. Keep correct grammar and avoid casual fragments that would undermine clarity.

Distinguish firmness from accusation. State the issue without assigning motives. Express a disagreement through the concrete concern, its impact, and the proposed resolution. Do not bury the request under excessive reassurance or flattery. Avoid repeated apologies or performed emotion, but keep a sincere apology when responsibility or the relationship warrants one.

A prior “emotionless” email direction applied to that particular email; it does not prohibit empathy in condolence messages, kindness to colleagues, appreciation, or warmth toward a friend. Never manufacture emotional claims Keith has not endorsed.

## Personal narrative and reflection

Keep the actual scene and the sequence of events. Keith's authentic samples can be candid about anxiety, discomfort, progress, and relief. Allow emotion when the supplied account supports it rather than assuming he is always detached.

Use details that explain what happened or why it mattered. A grounded, slightly self-aware aside can work. Do not invent sensory detail, dialogue, a friend, an identity marker, a past event, or a tidy lesson. If the conclusion is a broader cultural argument rather than personal growth, preserve that choice.

## Analytical, technical, and instructional writing

Carry over the mechanism-first reasoning: what is happening, why it works, how it changes the result. Define the relevant distinction before arguing from it. Use exact technical terms where needed and plain connective language around them.

Prefer a specific useful explanation over generic praise of the method. A short procedure can be steps or bullets; a complex explanation can use connected prose. Do not insist on essay paragraphs when the requested output is a checklist, report, documentation, or troubleshooting note. Technical genre coverage is an adaptation of the supported core, not proof of a large independently verified technical corpus.

## Presentations, scripts, and mixed formats

Separate what the audience sees from what Keith says. Prior requests support limited text on slides and more complete explanation in the spoken script. Formal wording and situational humor can coexist. Keep the script speakable; do not make every slide carry a paragraph or force jokes into a serious context.

## Creative and unfamiliar situations

Adapt the supported core to the chosen genre without pretending that a full creative-writing voice has been established. Generated books are not original Keith prose; his editing directions can inform preferences, but their narration is not a style exemplar by default.

If the user supplies a new sample for a new situation, weight that relevant sample heavily. Blend registers when needed: an email to a familiar professor may be conversational-professional, and a research presentation may be technical-professional. The result should fit its reader before it advertises a personal style.
