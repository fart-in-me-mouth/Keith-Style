# Sentence, grammar, and reasoning patterns

These are tendencies supported by the corpus, not numerical targets. Use the ones that serve the current thought. For register-specific changes, see [registers.md](registers.md).

## 1. The sentence follows the explanation

Keith frequently puts the claim and its justification in the same sentence. A clause opens another causal or qualifying clause instead of ending merely because the sentence is long.

Common structures, expressed as abstractions rather than authentic quotations:

- `[Claim] because [mechanism], which [consequence].`
- `When [condition], [effect], making [further result] more likely.`
- `Although [valid counterpoint], [position], because [limiting distinction].`
- `[Concrete example]. This [explains or changes something specific].`
- `[Agreement on the problem]. Where I disagree is [method or proposed consequence].`

The signature is the relationship between clauses, not the presence of the word *because*. Every added clause should advance the reasoning. Repair a chain if the subject shifts invisibly, a pronoun becomes ambiguous, or two distinct ideas lose their boundaries. Preserve it if it is merely less symmetrical than an editor might prefer.

He also uses decisive short statements. They usually land a judgment or move to the next practical point; they should not turn a whole essay into staccato copywriting. In professional exposition, keep an ordinary claim and its explanation in a coherent paragraph rather than separating them to perform emphasis. Preserve genuine unevenness in an existing user draft when requested, but do not generate unevenness as a professional voice marker.

## 2. Repeat the subject when clarity benefits

The earlier rhetorical analysis repeats *author, audience, nature,* and *purpose*. The performance-anxiety material repeats the named problem rather than searching for stylistic substitutes. This keeps the argument attached to its subject and sometimes creates a slightly insistent cadence.

Keep purposeful repetition across a causal sequence. Cut redundant restatement, repeated setup, and empty intensifiers. Do not erase an important distinction merely to reduce a word count.

## 3. Explain why the evidence matters

A typical analytical paragraph does more than name a technique or offer an example:

1. Identify the author's move or a concrete event.
2. Explain what it makes a reader think or feel, or how the event operates.
3. Connect that effect to the larger claim or purpose.

For persuasive writing, the comparable movement is policy/proposal → practical operation → consequence → judgment. For technical writing, it is observation → mechanism → implication. Avoid stopping at “this shows” without saying what it shows and why. In an article, “This is important” is not a substitute for that explanation; state the particular evidentiary limit, consequence, or causal relationship instead. This is a substantive check, not a blacklist of transitions.

## 4. Concession is a real distinction, not ceremonial balance

Keith can acknowledge a problem or a reasonable objection without conceding the solution. His privacy essay revisions focus on agreeing that children need protection while disputing the method. His older books essay addresses possible objections and answers them.

Do not let every paragraph become claim → “but” → qualification. A concession answers an actual objection; an evidentiary limit defines what a claim supports. Neither needs to recur as a stylistic signature. State each where the reasoning requires it, including repeated local qualifications when omitting them would overstate separate claims.

Use a concession when it represents the actual position. Do not bolt a “while both sides have merit” paragraph onto a settled point. Do not call a proposed solution a contradiction merely because it responds to a problem. Strong criticism still needs a valid target.

## 5. Lexical preferences are functional

Stronger evidence supports moving from inflated or distracting phrases to ordinary, exact descriptions: *manage* instead of a more awkward *reasonably solve* construction; stating that access is granted instead of a Fort Knox joke; naming personal information instead of a “distant public bulletin board.”

This is not a blacklist of long words. Authentic older prose contains formal and sometimes unusual constructions. In a relevant context, terms such as *conservation, personification,* or *cumulative explanation* may be more Keith-like than vague simplification. Keep specificity; remove performative sophistication.

Avoid default filler such as announcing that a topic is important, describing a discussion as nuanced before supplying the nuance, or explaining that a personal account was a profound learning journey. These fail because they replace his point, not because every occurrence of a phrase is forbidden.

## 6. Emphasis has a job

Conversational Keith sometimes uses capitals, repetition, and words such as *really, genuinely,* or *actually* to stress the exact part an assistant missed. His academic prose can also emphasize a selected word. The function is corrective or assertive, not decoration.

In polished work, use the sentence's argument and word order before adding capitals. In casual chat, an occasional emphatic word can be natural. Do not scatter intensifiers through every sentence or invent a universal prohibition on them.

## 7. Humor is context-dependent

His technology narrative uses mock-grandiose descriptions of childhood gaming and occasional asides. In later argument revisions, he removes assistant-created jokes and metaphors that distract from the point. Both are evidence: he can be funny, but does not want an assistant to manufacture constant personality.

Keep humor arising from a real supplied situation or his own phrase. Do not reuse gamer anecdotes, jokes, or unrelated beliefs as a signature in every draft.

## 8. Grammar should preserve voice without simulating mistakes

The original samples include awkward attachments, long sentences, repeated words, inconsistent mechanics, and nonstandard phrasing. His explicit editing requests distinguish basic correction from replacing his whole structure.

Preserve intentional word order and recognizable clause connections. Fix mistakes appropriate to the requested editing depth. Do not automatically turn every sentence into textbook parallelism. Conversely, never introduce a misspelling, comma splice, or agreement error solely to make new writing appear “human.”

Use contractions naturally in conversation and many personal essays. Formality may call for fewer; there is no evidence for a blanket rule. In comparable essay editing, prefer no em dashes because of explicit essay instructions. Other genres have no established blanket restriction; follow the current task and user direction. Punctuation should follow the sentence, not generic anti-AI folklore.

## 9. Openings and endings are not fixed templates

Keith describes beginning with a point and sometimes breaking it into three thesis points. He also identifies introductions as awkward and prefers getting to his disagreement quickly. This supports a practical opening, not an immutable three-part thesis requirement.

A professional informational opening should establish the subject and relevant relationship directly. Accessibility does not require an audience-correcting hook or a spoken series of questions. See the current calibration example in [examples.md](examples.md); do not treat one unsuccessful opening as evidence that every question, short sentence, or later passage is wrong.

An ending can move from the argument to its actual implication. Do not recap every paragraph just to signal closure. Do not add “I realized I must become better” unless that is his real conclusion. Personal reflection can include growth, uncertainty, relief, or vulnerability when his source material genuinely does.

## 10. Compress waste, not substance

Keith explicitly distinguishes cutting wordiness from simply cutting word count. A category plus an unnecessary itemized list may be redundant; a specific personal detail or causal explanation may be essential. Evaluate each sentence's role: new claim, support, distinction, mechanism, consequence, request, or necessary context. Remove what has no role. Do not shorten away the reason the argument works.
